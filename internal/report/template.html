How Claude is paid for, in plain words

Anthropic explains this in about six different places and never all at once. Here it is in one go. Nothing below is specific to you, so it is safe to share.

1. What the company buys

The company pays one bill each month. It buys a seat for every person, and that is it. The bill does not go up when you use Claude more, and it does not go down when you use it less. It is the same number either way.

There are two sizes of seat. A Standard seat is the normal one. A Premium seat costs about five times more and comes with a much bigger monthly allowance, so it goes to people who work with Claude all day.

2. So what is the number on your dashboard?

It is your share of that one bill, worked out from how much you actually used compared with everyone else. Add up every colleague's number and you get the bill exactly. Not a cent more.

Two things follow, and they are the whole point of this dashboard:

  • If your number is bigger than your own seat costs, you are getting more out of Claude than your licence alone paid for. Most seats in any company go barely used, and that spare capacity gets counted towards the people who do turn up. That is a good thing, not a problem.
  • If your number is smaller, someone else is making use of the capacity your seat paid for. Also fine. It just means there is room for you to do more.

You are never billed for any of it. There is no invoice coming to you, no budget you are overspending. It is a way of seeing where the money the company already spent is actually going.

3. The limits: three of them, and they are separate

Your seat comes with an allowance, not unlimited use. The allowance is policed by three different counters. Think of them as three taps that can each run dry on their own.

CounterWhat it isWhen it resetsWhat happens if you hit it
5-hour window A short-term brake, so nobody can burn a week of capacity in one morning. Five hours after your first message in that window. You wait. Usually not long. Go and do something else.
Weekly allowance The one that really matters. Two counters actually: one for everything, and a stricter one just for the most expensive model. A fixed day and time each week, the same one every week for your account. You are out until the reset, which could be days away. This is the one that ruins an afternoon.
Monthly spend cap Only ever comes into play once the two above are used up, and only if the company has switched on usage credits. First day of the month. Genuinely stopped until next month, or until an owner raises the cap.
They are not alternatives, they are all live at once. Any single one of them can block you while the others still have room. Plenty of weekly allowance left does not help if your five-hour window is full.

4. It is all one pot

Chat in the browser, Cowork on your desktop, Claude Code in a terminal, the Word and Excel add-ins: they all draw from the same allowance. There is no separate budget per app.

So an afternoon of heavy coding can leave you unable to ask a simple question in the browser that evening. If that keeps happening, it is worth knowing which of your habits is eating the allowance, which is exactly what the Sessions tab shows.

5. What happens when you actually run out

If usage credits are switched on for your plan, hitting your weekly allowance does not stop you dead. You carry on working and the extra use is paid for from a prepaid balance.

That is not a blank cheque, and it is not there to be burned through. Every person has their own credit budget, set the same way for everyone on the same seat type, so the extra capacity is shared fairly rather than going to whoever shouts loudest. There is also a ceiling for the whole company each month.

If you genuinely need more room, ask. Do not sit blocked, and do not quietly work around it either. Request more credit and if the reason is reasonable your personal budget gets raised. That is the intended path, not an exception. What it is not for is leaving an agent running unattended overnight, or repeating the same expensive job because it was easier than fixing the prompt.

6. Why cost and limits do not move together

This one is genuinely confusing and worth thirty seconds. Every time Claude answers, it re-reads the whole conversation so far. In a long session that re-reading is well over 90% of everything it processes.

The bill appears to care mostly about what Claude writes back, so the re-reading barely shows up in the cost figures here. Your allowance looks like it counts the re-reading too. That combination means a very long session can eat your weekly allowance while hardly moving your cost. If you keep running into limits but your dashboard number looks modest, this is why.

7. What to actually do about it

  • Finish a topic, then start a new session. The single most effective habit. A long session gets more expensive with every message, because there is more to re-read each time. Starting fresh resets that.
  • Match the model to the job. The most capable model can cost ten times the cheapest one. Use it for hard thinking, not for reformatting a list.
  • Watch the Sessions tab for the yellow marks. Those are sessions that ran long enough for the cost per message to climb steeply. A few of those usually explain most of a heavy month.
  • Do not ration yourself out of caution. The seat is already paid for. Unused allowance is wasted money, not saved money.

What this shows, and what it cannot

Where the numbers come from. Claude Cowork and Claude Code write a transcript of every session onto your own machine, and each assistant reply carries the exact token counts Anthropic returned. This page reads those transcripts, throws away the duplicate entries that streaming produces, and prices what is left. The token counts are exact, not estimated.

What is missing. claude.ai in the browser and on mobile keeps no local transcript, so none of that usage appears here. There is deliberately no way to pull it in: doing so would mean handing round an organisation-wide export of everyone's activity, and this dashboard is meant to stay yours. If you work mostly in the browser, this page will show less than your real usage, and that is the trade.

Nothing here is money you owe. Your seat is a flat fee. These figures show what your work costs the organisation, and what it would cost if it were metered. Both are for deciding things, not for settling up.

macOS and Linux builds are untested. Both platforms build and run in browser mode (no app window, since WebView2 is Windows-only), but neither has been run on real hardware. Report anything odd.

The two cost bases

Subscription, what we pay. The default. Your usage as a share of what the organisation actually pays Anthropic each month. Every euro of the invoice is allocated by consumption, so all the personal dashboards together add up to the real invoice and nothing more.

Why your figure can exceed your own seat price. This surprises people, so it is worth stating plainly. The organisation buys a seat for everyone, but most of those seats go almost unused. That paid-for capacity does not vanish; the allocation hands it to whoever actually consumed it. So if you are among the heavier users, your share is several times your own seat price, and if you barely use Claude your share is a fraction of it. Both are correct, and neither changes the invoice by a cent. Read a high number as "I am getting a lot out of the licences we already bought", not as "I am costing extra".

API list price. Every token at Anthropic's published rates, cache traffic included. It runs several times higher than the subscription figure. It is the right number for quoting client work or sizing an API build, and the wrong number for reporting cost.

How the subscription figure is derived

Anthropic's Team consumption meter behaves as though cache traffic is not charged. That was established by fitting against their own reported per-person spend: every pricing model that charged for cache came out four to eight and a half times too high, while an output-driven model landed within about a fifth. Since cache reads are roughly 94% of raw token volume, that single distinction dominates everything else.

So the subscription basis prices output tokens at each model's output rate and scales the result by one calibration factor, chosen so that a known heavy user's figure matches what Anthropic reports for the same window. Then the organisation's whole monthly subscription is allocated across consumption.

Honest limits. The factor is fitted, not published. It was calibrated against one person's usage mix over 30 days, so someone with a very different mix (mostly browser chat, or mostly Haiku) will be somewhat off. It also allocates the cost of idle seats onto the people who do use Claude, which is deliberate: that cost is real and has to land somewhere. Treat these figures as accurate to within a fifth or so, not to the cent.

Reading a marathon session

An agent session re-sends its entire context on every turn. Across a large measured sample, median cost per call rose from about $0.057 in the first 25 calls to $0.269 by call 300, because median context grew from 98K to 490K tokens. Nothing went wrong; the session simply got heavy.

So the lever is not the model you pick, it is session length. Finish a topic, start a new session for the next one. That single habit moves the number more than anything else you can do.

List prices used

USD per million tokens. Cache write is 1.25× input, cache read is 0.1× input. Update the price table in the extract script when Anthropic's list changes.

How accurate is this?

Close enough to act on, not exact. This dashboard was built with Claude, and Anthropic does not publish how usage turns into money. So the figures come from comparing measured token counts against the totals Anthropic reports for the organisation. They land within roughly a fifth.

Three things pull them off. The calibration is fitted to one heavy user's mix of models, so a very different pattern of use will be somewhat out. Your session files can be up to a day old. And prices change, sometimes mid-month.

None of that affects what you would actually use this for. The trend is right, the split between Cowork and Code is right, and the ranking of your sessions from cheap to expensive is right. Treat it as a compass, not an invoice.

Two token counts here are exact, for what it is worth: the number of API calls and the number of tokens. Anthropic returns both on every reply. It is only the conversion into euros that is estimated.

Refreshing

This page holds a snapshot. It cannot poll anything by itself: no network access, no way to read your files.

You have automatic refresh set up. It runs on a schedule and republishes this page. You can also press Run now on that task in the Scheduled sidebar, or type /usage-dashboard in Cowork, to rebuild it on demand.

This page is rebuilt every time you run the burnmon CLI again. It reads your session transcripts live at each run, so refreshing is simply running it again: a new report is written and opened for you.
